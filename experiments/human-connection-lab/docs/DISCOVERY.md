# Discovery — how should people find each other on the web?

This is the record of how the concept was chosen: the problem as framed, the
directions explored, how they were judged, and why one won. Rejected directions
are kept on purpose, with the reason they lost, so the decision can be argued
with later instead of re-derived.

## 1. The problem, reframed

"Help people find each other" sounds like a search problem. It mostly is not.
There are billions of people online; nobody lacks candidates. What fails is the
**middle**: the passage from *stranger* to *someone I'd recognise and keep
talking to*. Existing products pour their effort into the first contact (the
swipe, the follow, the match) and then hand people an empty text box and walk
away.

What is known about how acquaintance actually becomes friendship points at a
small number of mechanisms:

- **Reciprocal, gradually deepening self‑disclosure.** Closeness comes from
  disclosure that is *matched* — I say a little, you say a little, we go one
  step deeper together (Altman & Taylor's social penetration theory; Aron et al.'s
  "36 questions" study produced measurable closeness between strangers in 45
  minutes using exactly this structure).
- **Repeated contact over time.** Hall (2018) estimates roughly 50 hours
  together to go from acquaintance to casual friend. One great conversation is
  not a friendship; a rhythm is.
- **A reason to talk that is not "present yourself".** Shared activity and
  shared prompts remove the blank page.
- **Safety from exposure.** People hold back because going first is risky: the
  first to open up, the first to say "let's keep talking", the first to ask for
  a name risks being left exposed alone.

## 2. Why the familiar patterns fail at the middle

| Pattern | What it gets wrong |
|---|---|
| Profiles | Self‑presentation before contact. People are judged on a curated surface (mostly a photo), which rewards performance and punishes the quiet. |
| Swiping / browsing | Turns people into inventory. Choice overload, near‑zero cost of rejection, near‑zero investment. |
| Feeds & followers | Broadcast, not relationship. Optimised for attention, measured in counts. |
| Open chat | The blank page ("hey"), asymmetric effort, threads that never end and never resolve, ghosting as the default ending. |
| Rooms & forums | Crowd dynamics: the loudest set the tone, the newcomer watches. |
| Interest matching | Interest overlap is a weak predictor of friendship; it produces small talk about the interest. |

The shared root: **every one of them makes somebody go first, alone.** First to
present, first to write, first to be vulnerable, first to propose more, first
to leave. Whoever goes first carries all the risk, so most people do not.

## 3. Directions explored

Seven genuinely different directions, each pushed until its real weakness
showed.

**A. Do something together.** Two strangers complete a small cooperative task
(a puzzle, a drawing, a shared playlist) and conversation grows out of the
activity. *Strength:* no blank page; character shows through action. *Weakness:*
quickly becomes a game product where the game is the point; skill gaps embarrass;
does nothing for the second, third, tenth contact.

**B. Slow letters.** Asynchronous letters with delivery delay. *Strength:*
depth, care, anticipation. *Weakness:* already exists (Slowly, pen‑pal sites);
the long letter is a heavy first step and still makes someone write first.

**C. Standing appointments.** The unit is a recurring meeting: the same small
table of four people, ten minutes a week, for a six‑week season. *Strength:*
built directly on "repetition makes friends"; bounded commitment. *Weakness:*
a large up‑front commitment to strangers; needs synchronous availability;
critical mass is hard; the first session is still four strangers and a blank
page.

**D. Same moment, elsewhere.** Connect people who are in the same moment right
now in different places — both awake at 3am, both watching rain. *Strength:*
emotionally striking, very little effort. *Weakness:* ephemeral by nature;
needs live density; produces beautiful one‑offs, not relationships.

**E. Help as the seed.** People post small asks and offers ("proofread my
letter", "I can teach sourdough"); helping is the introduction. *Strength:* a
concrete reason to meet; the Ben Franklin effect. *Weakness:* drifts toward a
transactional marketplace; relationship framed as service.

**F. A shared object that grows.** Each relationship is a shared artefact (a
garden, a map) that grows when both contribute and fades when neglected.
*Strength:* makes the relationship itself visible. *Weakness:* streak mechanics
— guilt as retention — are corrosive (see Snapchat streaks); decoration risks
becoming the product.

**G. Simultaneous, sealed reciprocity — "nobody goes first".** Every step of a
connection is made of two sealed halves. You write your half without seeing
theirs; they write theirs without seeing yours; both open at the same moment.
The same rule governs discovery (you read strangers' answers only after you
have written yours), conversation (every exchange is sealed until both have
written), identity (a name is revealed only if both chose to reveal), and every
decision to continue (a one‑sided "no" is never shown to anyone).

## 4. Evaluation

Scored 1–5 against the mission's criteria. The scores are judgment, not
measurement; the reasoning under the table matters more than the numbers.

| | Original | Improves connection | Easy to grasp | Want to return | Meaningful, not superficial | Buildable v1 | Can grow | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Do together | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 22 |
| B. Slow letters | 1 | 4 | 5 | 3 | 5 | 5 | 3 | 26 |
| C. Standing appointments | 3 | 5 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 4 | 25 |
| D. Same moment | 4 | 2 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 21 |
| E. Help as seed | 2 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 23 |
| F. Shared object | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 3 | 21 |
| **G. Nobody goes first** | **4** | **5** | **4** | **4** | **5** | **5** | **5** | **32** |

Why G wins:

1. **It attacks the root cause rather than a symptom.** Section 2 found one
   failure shared by every familiar pattern — someone has to go first, alone.
   G removes it structurally, not with encouragement copy.
2. **It encodes the actual mechanism of closeness.** Sealed pairs make
   disclosure reciprocal and matched by construction. You cannot overshare into
   silence; you cannot be the only one who answered honestly.
3. **One rule, applied everywhere.** Discovery, conversation, identity and
   endings all follow the same principle, so the product is learnable from one
   example. That coherence is what separates a product from a feature list.
4. **It is honest about time.** Conversations are grouped into short chapters
   with a sealed "keep going?" at each end — bounded commitment (the strength of
   C) without its up‑front cost, and graceful endings instead of ghosting.
5. **It is demonstrable now.** The core loop works with locally simulated
   people without pretending to be anything it is not.

What it borrows deliberately: the bounded rhythm of C (chapters), the
anticipation of B (the sealed half), and the "reason to talk" of A (every
exchange begins with a question, never a blank box).

## 5. Weaknesses of the chosen direction, faced up front

- **Waiting.** Sealed exchanges mean you sometimes wait for the other half.
  This is accepted deliberately: the wait is the anticipation that makes
  opening matter. It must never feel broken, so every waiting state says what
  is happening and what you can do meanwhile.
- **Inference of a "no".** If I chose to continue and the chapter "came to
  rest", I can infer the other person did not. The rule cannot hide that; it
  can make sure nobody is ever *told* "they said no", and that the moment
  reads as a gentle ending rather than a rejection.
- **Prompt fatigue.** Questions can become a quiz. Mitigations: questions are
  written to invite stories rather than facts, there are few per chapter, and
  the person whose turn it is chooses between options.
- **Text‑first excludes some people.** Voice halves are the obvious next
  medium (Section "Open questions" in PRODUCT.md).
- **Simulation limits.** In this first version the other people are simulated
  locally; they cannot read what you write. This is stated in the product, not
  hidden.

## 6. Ideas rejected inside the chosen direction

- **Typing indicators.** Rejected: they reintroduce the pressure of "they're
  watching me write" and would have to be faked in a demo.
- **Showing the length of a sealed half.** Rejected: length leaks effort and
  invites comparison before opening.
- **Streaks or counters of any kind.** Rejected: guilt is not a reason to
  return.
- **Photos at first contact.** Rejected: the product exists to let people meet
  through how they think before how they look.
- **Unlimited "leaning in" on strangers' answers.** Rejected: three per day
  keeps each one intentional.
